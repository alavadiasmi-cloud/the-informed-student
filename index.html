The student perspective · Research snapshot

Being informed is more than knowing the headline.

It means asking questions, checking sources, understanding context and joining the conversation.

01 / The research

What does it mean to be an informed student?

This exploratory survey looked at students’ news habits, preferred sources, and confidence engaging with current affairs.

The question behind the project

Are we seeing the news—or understanding it?

News is easy to encounter in a social feed. Understanding its context, evaluating the source and discussing its implications can take more effort.

The project aims to make thoughtful engagement with current affairs feel more accessible, practical and relevant to students.

02 / Key findings

A gap worth paying attention to.

Survey responses point to a useful question: how can students turn exposure to news into understanding and confident participation?

Finding 01

Social platforms are a common entry point.

Instagram was the most frequently selected news source in this sample. Social platforms can help students encounter stories, but a post alone may not provide the full context.

Instagram as most-used source63%
Finding 02

Many respondents spend limited time with news.

Most respondents reported spending less than 15 minutes a day consuming news. Short, intentional habits may be more approachable than expecting students to follow every development.

Less than 15 minutes daily70%
Finding 03

Feeling informed is not the same as feeling confident.

Only 23% rated their confidence discussing political issues at 4–5 out of 5, while 67% rated responsible citizenship and staying informed at 4–5.

High confidence discussing politics23%
Finding 04

International awareness may need more support.

40% rated themselves highly informed about major international events and conflicts, compared with 17% for political developments in India. These are self-assessments, not verified knowledge scores.

What the findings suggest

Move from consumption to participation.

The survey does not show that students lack interest in public life. It suggests a practical opportunity: make it easier to understand a story, check what supports it, and talk about what it means.

You do not have to know everything to be an informed citizen. Start with one issue, ask better questions, and follow the evidence.

A 15-minute routine

Make room for understanding.

A simple routine to try a few times a week. Adjust it to your schedule and interests.

Choose one story

Spend 3 minutes selecting a report from a source you trust. Pick an issue you genuinely want to understand.

Read for context

Spend 8 minutes reading beyond the headline. Identify the key actors, background, evidence and what remains uncertain.

Reflect or discuss

Spend 4 minutes writing one question, checking a second source or discussing the story with someone else.
